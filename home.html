Car Crash Simulation

Send a detailed model of a real car into a rigid barrier or a brick wall and watch the crash unfold in slow motion. The structure crumples, panels tear off, glass shatters and a crash-test dummy rides it out, with injury numbers for each test. Six crash labs go further: offset, two-car, side, rear, restraint and pedestrian tests. Everything runs in your web browser.

Overlap

Frontal overlap lab

  • Lexus RX 350
  • 10–50 mph
  • 40% or 25% overlap
  • Rigid or honeycomb

Hit the barrier with only part of the front. A 40% overlap loads a main frame rail; a 25% small overlap misses it and drives the wheel back toward the footwell. Follow the crash pulse and the steering-column, pedal and toe-pan intrusion, each checked against its limit.

Two cars

Two-vehicle collision lab

  • Two cars
  • 0–60 mph each
  • Head-on to 45°
  • Mass mismatch

Two cars meet head-on or at an angle. They share the momentum, so the lighter car changes speed the most. Compare each driver’s speed change (Δv) and injuries, and watch the kinetic energy turn into bent metal and heat.

What it simulates

The crash is a physics computation, not a canned animation. Change the speed, the angle or the car and the outcome changes with it.

A structure that crumples

Each car is a lattice of 700 to 1,000 nodes joined by springs that yield and stay bent. It has a soft crumple zone, a stiff passenger cell and an engine block. The 3D model is bound to the lattice, so it dents where the metal does.

Parts, glass and tyres

Bumpers, hood, fenders, doors and mirrors tear off when their mounts give way, and fly off in the shape they were crushed into. Side windows and lamps shatter, the windshield cracks, tyres burst and wheels break away.

A crash-test dummy

A seated 3D dummy feels the cabin's deceleration and the interior pushed in around it, held by a three-point belt with pretensioner and force limiter, and by an airbag. It reports head injury criterion (HIC), chest acceleration and deflection, and neck and thigh loads, against standard limits.

Your test conditions

Speed from 10 to 150 km/h, an approach angle up to ±45°, vehicle mass and front-end stiffness. Choose a rigid barrier or a brick wall with three mortar strengths, and Realistic or Dramatic damage.

Slow motion and analysis

Bullet-time slows the replay to 1/40 speed around the hardest moment of the crash, with camera shake and 3D sound, and you can save it as a video. Five cameras, including an onboard view of the dummy. Charts show the crash pulse, crush and an energy breakdown. A strain map colours the car by how much it has bent.

Runs in your browser

No install and no server: one HTML file with WebGL graphics. The physics is plain JavaScript, and an automated test suite checks every change: no energy appears from nowhere, momentum is kept when parts break off, and cars that are parked or coasting behave.

How a test runs

The same four stages as a real crash test, from setup to the high-speed footage.

  1. Set up

    Choose the car, speed, angle, barrier, damage level and restraints. Drag the handle in the 3D view to aim the approach.

  2. Approach

    An automated driver, with cruise control and steering, brings the car up to speed and holds it on its line. An emergency stop brakes the car, or cancels the run while the impact is computed.

  3. Impact

    The crash is computed in 0.1-millisecond steps, fine enough to resolve a crash pulse that lasts about a tenth of a second. It takes one to a few seconds.

  4. Replay

    Watch in slow motion, scrub frame by frame, switch cameras and read the results. You can also re-run the dummy with or without its belt and airbag.

Vehicles

Two real cars, split into their actual panels, plus a simple reference car.

Lexus RX 350

Midsize SUV · 1,950 kg

The default car. Its bumpers, hood, fenders, four doors, tailgate, mirrors, lamps and nine glass panes are all separate parts.

Ford Mustang GT500

Coupe · 1,890 kg

A low sports coupe with a long front overhang: a longer crumple zone, a softer crash pulse and a visible engine when the hood comes off.

Lab sedan

Procedural reference · 1,500 kg

A plain generated body that deforms but has no separate parts. It is the physics baseline that every change is checked against.

About the numbers. Car Crash Simulation is an independent project for demonstration and teaching. Its physics follows real crash-test practice, but it hasn't been validated against physical crash tests. Use it to compare settings and see trends, not to predict real-world injuries.
